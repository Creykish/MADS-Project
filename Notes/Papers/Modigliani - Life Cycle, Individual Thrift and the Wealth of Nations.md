## Modigliani - Life Cycle, Individual Thrift and the Wealth of Nations

**Source:** Lecture based on 1985 Economic Sciences Prize

---

## Why This Paper (For Your Research)

This is **the foundational text** for lifecycle asset allocation theory. It establishes:
- Why households accumulate wealth during working years and decumulate during retirement
- The relationship between saving, growth, and the wealth-income ratio
- How to think about optimal consumption and portfolio decisions over a lifetime
- The limitations and extensions of simple consumption rules

It's essential background for understanding why lifecycle glidepaths exist and what the theoretical justification is.

---

## Part I: Historical Antecedents

### The Keynesian Problem (1930s-1950s)

Before the Life Cycle Hypothesis, the dominant view was crude and empirical:
- **Consumption as function of current income only** (Keynes' "law")
- Saving was assumed to be the preserve of the rich (bequest motive)
- Oversaving was seen as a threat to economic stability
- No theoretical framework for *why* households choose to save

### Key Empirical Anomalies (1940s)

Three landmark studies broke the old model:

1. **Kuznets [1946]:** Saving ratio stable despite 100+ years of income growth
   - Implication: saving is not simply a luxury good rising with income

2. **Brady & Friedman [1947]:** Saving depends on *relative* income, not absolute
   - Families save based on income relative to mean income of their cohort
   - Resolved Kuznets paradox but raised new questions

3. **Margaret Reid (unpublished):** Consumption responds to *permanent* (normal) income, not current
   - Directly inspired both LCH (Modigliani) and PIH (Friedman 1957)

## Part II: The Life Cycle Hypothesis

### Core Principle: Utility Maximization Over a Finite Life

**Key insight:** A rational consumer smooths consumption over their lifetime based on *total life resources*, not current income.

**Life Resources** = present value of:
- Future labor income
- Bequests received (if any)

**Implication:** Saving in any given year = (Life Resources - Current Consumption) / Remaining Life

When current income > average life consumption -> positive saving (accumulation)
When current income < average life consumption -> negative saving (decumulation)

---

### The "Stripped-Down" Model: Five Key Simplifications

To isolate core mechanisms, Modigliani assumes:

1. **Income:** Constant until retirement at age 65, zero thereafter
2. **Interest rate:** Zero (for tractability)
3. **Consumption:** Constant in real terms over entire life
4. **Bequests:** None
5. **Life expectancy:** Known with certainty

### The Result: The Hump-Shaped Wealth Path (Figure 1)

**Wealth trajectory by age:**
- Age 0-25: Low wealth, high consumption relative to income (if borrowing allowed)
- Age 25-65: Wealth rises → peak at retirement
- Age 65-death: Wealth declines → zero at death (pure LCH)

---

### Six Fundamental Propositions

1. **Saving rate is independent of per capita income level**
   - A poor country and rich country with same growth rate save the same % of income
   - Implication: Differences in national saving are *not* about thrift, but about demographics/growth

2. **National saving rates differ by growth rate, not citizen behavior**
   - Countries with identical individual lifecycle behavior can have very different aggregate saving rates

3. **Higher growth → higher national saving rate**
   - Saving rate = (M/2) × growth rate, where M = length of retirement
   - At zero growth, national saving = 0 (dissaving of old offsets accumulation of young)

4. **Wealth-income ratio inversely related to growth**
   - Slower growth → higher W/Y ratio
   - Zero growth → largest W/Y ratio

5. **Substantial wealth can accumulate without bequests**
   - Pure lifecycle saving alone can generate W/Y ≈ 4-5 (historical U.S. level)
   - Bequests are not necessary to explain observed wealth stocks

6. **Retirement length is the key control parameter**
   - W/Y = (M/2) where M = years in retirement
   - Longer retirement → higher required saving

---

### The Aggregate Consumption Function

Short-run relationship:

$$C_t = \alpha Y_L + \delta W_{t-1}$$

Where:
- $C_t$ = consumption
- $Y_L$ = labor income
- $W_{t-1}$ = wealth lagged one period
- $\alpha$, $\delta$ = stable parameters determined by life parameters (age structure, life expectancy, growth)

**Implication:** Wealth (not just current income) drives short-run consumption, so monetary policy can work through asset prices, not just investment.

For standard assumptions (L=50 years working, M=10 years retired, ρ=3% growth), δ ≈ 0.07.

## Part III: Relaxing the Simplifying Assumptions

### Non-Zero Interest Rates

Effect on saving:
- Income effect: Higher r → higher lifetime income → higher consumption → *lower* saving
- Substitution effect: Higher r → future consumption more expensive → lower current consumption → *higher* saving

**Modigliani's conclusion:** These likely offset, so **interest rates have little effect on aggregate saving** (empirically supported to date, though hotly debated).

*Exception:* Pension-based saving through annuities may show different interest rate sensitivity in long run.

### Realistic Income Profiles

Real income is **hump-shaped**, peaking in mid-50s, then declining.

Consumption also varies with **family size** (hump-shaped, earlier peak).

**Result:** When both are hump-shaped and roughly aligned, saving rates are fairly stable through the working years, with dissaving in youth and old age.

**Peak wealth:** Empirically observed around age 60–65 ✓ (matches theory)

### Variable Retirement Length

Household can choose retirement length endogenously.

In principle, richer economies could work longer (higher opportunity cost) or retire earlier (luxury good).

**Empirical finding:** No strong correlation between income level and retirement length, so Proposition 1 holds approximately.

### Liquidity Constraints

Imperfect credit markets prevent unconstrained borrowing against future income.

**Effect:** Postpones consumption, raises W/Y and saving rates.

**Important for:** Understanding why young households with low current income may save less than the model predicts.

### Myopia

Households might be too short-sighted to save adequately for retirement.

**Evidence against myopia:** Peak wealth in the U.S. is 5+ times average income, which is consistent with lifecycle smoothing of consumption through retirement. If myopic, peak wealth would be much lower.

## Part IV: Bequests and the Bequest Motive

### How Much Wealth is Inherited?

**Modigliani's estimate [1985]:** ~20% (±5pp) of total wealth is inherited in the U.S.

**Ando & Kennickell [1985]:** ~25% post-1974; ~30-40% pre-1974 (likely biased upward by capital gains).

**Kotlikoff & Summers [1981]:** >80% (highly controversial; shown to contain errors and use unconventional definitions).

**Implication:** The **overwhelming majority (~75-80%)** of wealth is self-accumulated through lifecycle saving, NOT inherited.

### Slow Decumulation in Old Age: Two Explanations

Observed fact: Retired households decumulate wealth *slowly*, not matching pure LCH prediction.

1. **Precautionary motive:** Uncertainty about lifespan means households hold buffer wealth; no perfect annuity market.
   - Cannot buy lifetime annuities easily (adverse selection, high costs)
   - Results in death with non-zero wealth as "unintended bequest"
   - This is NOT a bequest *motive*, just prudence

2. **Bequest motive:** Genuine desire to leave estate to heirs.
   - Concentrated in top income brackets
   - ~3% of households cite bequest as saving motive; ~33% of top wealth decile do
   - Menchik & David [1983]: Bequests rise sharply above 80th percentile of life resources

### Bequest Motive Consistent with LCH?

**Yes, if:**

**BI:** Bequest share depends on household's life resources *relative to cohort mean*, not absolute level.

**BII:** Distribution of relative life resources is stable over time.

Under these conditions, **all Propositions 1-5 still hold** even with significant bequests.

## Part V: Policy Implications

### 1. Short-Run Stabilization

**Monetary policy channel:** Changes in asset values (stock market, real estate) affect wealth → affect consumption via δW term in aggregate consumption function.

**Fiscal policy (transitory taxes):** Temporary tax cuts have small effects on consumption because they don't affect life resources. Households smooth over the tax change.
- Empirically supported: 1960s tax rebates had weak consumption effects

### 2. Long-Run Fiscal Policy

**Deficit financing vs. tax financing:**
- LCH predicts **private saving is independent of government budget stance**
- Therefore, government deficits crowd out private capital 1:1
- Burden falls on future generations

Contrasts sharply with **Ricardian Equivalence** (Barro 1974), which predicts private saving rises to offset deficits.

**When is deficit spending not harmful?**
- If economy has slack (unemployment) and monetary policy accommodates → can raise income and saving
- If government spends on productive investment → future generations also benefit
- In open economies → foreign capital can partially offset crowding out (but future generations pay interest on foreign debt)

**Implication for NZ:** The debate over government deficits (health, education, infrastructure) hinges partly on whether LCH or Ricardian view is correct.

## Key Findings & Mechanisms

| Phenomenon | Mechanism | Supporting Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Stable national saving despite rising income | Growth-driven saving (Neisser & Bentzel effects) not income-driven | Kuznets [1946], cross-country data |
| Hump-shaped wealth by age | Accumulation phase (working) + decumulation phase (retired) | Age-wealth profiles: peak 60-65 ✓ |
| Saving ratio rises with growth | Younger savers dominate when economy grows | Steady-state predictions match 2-4% growth economies |
| Relative income predicts saving (not absolute) | Transitory income is noise; permanent income drives saving | Brady-Friedman [1947], budget studies |
| Wealth-income ratio ~4-5 without bequests | Pure lifecycle accumulation sufficient | Goldsmith [1956], wealth surveys |
| Slow old-age dissaving | Precautionary motive + incomplete annuity markets | Hurd [1986] recent panel data |
| Small bequest motive share | Concentrated in top decile; most wealth is self-accumulated | Menchik & David [1983] probate data |
| Interest rates don't much affect saving | Income & substitution effects roughly offset | Friedman-Warshawsky [1985] annuity data |

---

## Distinctive Insights (vs. Keynes / Friedman PIH)

1. **Finite life matters:** Unlike PIH (infinite horizon), LCH predicts dissaving in retirement and allows bequest analysis.

2. **Growth-dependent:** Productivity growth has different implication in LCH vs. PIH (Bentzel effect dominates in LCH; income effect dominates in PIH).

3. **Relative income, not absolute:** LCH framework naturally explains why saving depends on position in income distribution, not level.

4. **Wealth is an independent aggregate driver:** Short-run consumption responds to both income AND wealth (not predicted by simple Keynesian or PIH models).

## For Your NZ Research

### Direct Applications

1. **NZ Super as implicit wealth:**
   - $~800k present value of guaranteed income floor
   - Acts like bonds/annuity in the lifecycle framework
   - Justifies potentially higher equity allocation in financial portfolio than TDF rules suggest
   - **Reduces need for private wealth accumulation** (Proposition 5)

2. **Retirement length implications:**
   - NZ life expectancy ~82 years
   - Retirement from 65 → 20+ year horizon
   - Per Modigliani: M=20 → W/Y = 10 (much higher than U.S. historical 4-5)
   - Implication: NZ retirees need more accumulated wealth if working longer (higher M)

3. **Growth rate & saving:**
   - NZ growth ~2-2.5%
   - Should predict lower national saving than high-growth economies
   - But also lower W/Y ratio needed at retirement (lower M relative to life)

4. **Household composition matters:**
   - LCH predicts couple households save differently than singles
   - With different consumption needs + family size effects
   - Your data on NZ spending by household type should fit LCH framework

### Extensions Needed

1. **Your spending decline (2%/year) isn't addressed in pure LCH:**
   - Basic model assumes constant real consumption
   - Reid/Modigliani note consumption varies with family size
   - Your empirical spending trajectory should feed back into optimal glidepath calculation

2. **Bequest motive in NZ context:**
   - Is it stronger or weaker than U.S. (given NZ Super, housing costs)?
   - How does home equity factor in? (Not fully addressed in classic LCH)

3. **Uncertainty & medical expenses:**
   - Modigliani notes precautionary saving but doesn't model long-term care costs
   - NZ has public healthcare but private residential care costs are substantial
   - Should affect optimal decumulation path (slower if medical costs uncertain)
