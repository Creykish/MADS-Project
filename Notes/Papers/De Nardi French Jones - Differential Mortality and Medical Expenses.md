[[Differential Mortality, Uncertain Medical Expenses, and the Saving of Elderly Singles.pdf]]
## Reference
Mariacristina De Nardi, Eric French, & John Bailey Jones. (n.d.). _Differential Mortality, Uncertain Medical Expenses, and the Saving of Elderly Singles_. National Bureau of Economic Research.


## Summary

Modelling [[Life Cycle Wealth Trajectory]] to investigate whether longevity and medical expense risk can explain large asset holdings even at advanced ages. They extend to develop a model that consistent with the following key facts about the U.S. data: women live longer than men, rich people live longer than poor people, and healthy people live longer than sick people.

They find that the risk of living long and facing high [[Medical Expenses]] goes a long way toward explaining the elderly's savings decisions. Specifically, medical expenses that rise quickly with both age and permanent income can explain why the elderly singles, and especially the richest ones, run down their assets so slowly. They also find that [[Pensions & Social Security]] has a big impact on the elderly's savings.

Their main conclusion is that to correctly evaluate any policy reform affecting the elderly’s saving decisions, one needs to model accurately the [[Consumption Floor]]and, at a minimum, the average level of medical expenses by age and by permanent income.

**Medical expenses by age (bad health woman):**
- Age 70: $1,200/year  
- Age 100: $19,000/year

**Medical expenses are luxury good:**
- Age 95 woman, 20th pct income: $2,700/year
- Age 95 woman, 80th pct income: $16,000/year

**Life expectancy at 70:**
- Sick male, poor (20th pct): 6 years
- Healthy female, rich (80th pct): 17 years

## Main Result

Top income quintile median assets (age 74-81):
- Baseline model: $150k → $150k (flat)
- No medical expense risk: $150k → $140k  
- No medical expenses at all: $150k → $90k
- No consumption floor: $150k → $220k
- Equal survival probabilities: $150k → $140k

**Takeaway:** Average medical costs matter way more than the risk. Consumption floor affects even rich people.

## Why This Paper Matters

Previous work (Hubbard et al, Palumbo) understated medical expenses → predicted too much asset decline.

This paper: Better data (AHEAD) + flexible specification → fits actual slow asset decline.

Hubbard's model: 100-year-old spends only 15% of what this paper estimates.

## Method

Structural lifecycle model, method of simulated moments:
- No bequest motive (isolate medical/mortality effects)
- CRRA utility (γ=3), β=0.95
- Consumption floor $5k (SSI/Medicaid/food stamps)
- Match income-conditional asset profiles

Medical expenses: $hc_t = f(sex, health, age, income, \psi_t)$

Where $\psi_t$ has persistent (ξ) and transitory (ε) components.

Model passes overidentifying restrictions test (rare).
