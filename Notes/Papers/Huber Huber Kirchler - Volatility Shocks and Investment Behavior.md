## Summary

Artefactual field experiment with **202 finance professionals** (portfolio managers, advisors, traders) and **282 students**. Participants watch 100 daily stock returns unfold in real time and make portfolio allocation decisions every 20 returns. A volatility shock is introduced in period 3 (5x increase in standard deviation: 0.93% -> 4.99%). Shock direction manipulated: down, up, or net-zero (straight). Presentation format: price chart or return bar chart.

## Key Results

**Investment propensity:**
- Both groups: investment negatively associated with shock direction (down shock -> reduce holdings; up shock -> maintain or increase)
- Professionals respond to the *price change*, not to volatility per se
- Students invest less overall

**Risk perception:**
- Professionals: perceived risk increases similarly after ALL shock directions (up, down, or flat) -> they correctly identify a volatility increase as risky regardless of direction
- Students: risk perception driven by frequency of negative returns, not by volatility level -> upward shocks not perceived as riskier
- Key difference: professionals perceive volatility as risk; students perceive loss probability as risk

**Satisfaction:**
- Both groups: satisfaction positively correlated with shock direction (up -> more satisfied, down -> less satisfied)
- Separate from risk perception -> satisfaction and risk are independent psychological dimensions

**Forecasts:**
- Professionals: no difference between price and return presentation formats
- Students: more extreme price forecasts in return-chart condition following net-zero and upward shocks

## Behavioral Implication for [[100% Stock Portfolio]]

An investor holding 100% equities will regularly experience volatility shocks. Even when the shock is upward (no loss), professionals increase perceived risk and reduce holdings. This creates a behavioral implementation problem:

- Rational all-equity strategy requires holding through high-volatility episodes
- But investors (even sophisticated professionals) systematically reduce equity after volatility shocks
- Sequence-of-returns risk is therefore not just a mathematical problem but a behavioral one: investors sell at the worst times
- -> actual outcomes from all-equity strategies may fall short of theoretical predictions

## Distinction Between Professionals and Novices

- Professionals have better volatility perception (they correctly register a volatility increase regardless of direction)
- But this better perception may lead to worse outcomes in an all-equity context: they reduce holdings even after upward shocks
- Students' naive "loss frequency" heuristic is less calibrated but may accidentally produce better all-equity adherence (they don't panic at neutral or positive volatility spikes)
