## Reference

Love, D. A., Palumbo, M. G., & Smith, P. A. (2009). The trajectory of wealth in retirement. *Journal of Public Economics*, 93(1-2), 191-208.

**Annualized comprehensive wealth:** Total resources ÷ expected remaining years ÷ household size

= Constant amount household could afford to spend each year until death (in expectation)

**Comprehensive = Financial + Non-financial + Annuity-like:**
- Net worth (financial + housing - debt)
- Social Security
- DB pensions  
- Transfers (Food Stamps, SSI, etc.)

## The Finding

**Median annualized wealth RISES in retirement:**

Age 70-75 cohort (1998-2006):
- 1998: $32,800/person/year
- 2006: $42,200/person/year
- **+30% in 8 years**

Full retirement span (regression):
- Age 65: $25,600/person/year
- Age 90: $50,000/person/year
- **Nearly doubles**

## Why Rising?

Wealth balances decline slower than life expectancy shortens.

**Distribution:**
- ~50% saw annualized wealth rise >25% (1998-2006)
- ~12% saw decline >25%
- Heavily tilted toward increases

**Similar across:**
- Marital status
- Income groups
- Race, education, health (to varying degrees)

## Components (age 70-75 in 1998)

**1998 total: $493k**
- Annuity-like: $264k (54%) - mostly Social Security $177k
- Financial: $98k (20%)
- Non-financial: $130k (26%) - mostly housing $97k

**2006 total: $393k** (down 20%)
- Annuity-like: $169k (43%) - share down as life expectancy falls
- Financial: $82k (21%) - stable share
- Non-financial: $142k (36%) - share UP (housing gains)

Even though total wealth fell 20%, annualized wealth rose 30% because life expectancy fell faster.

## Survivorship Bias

**Concern:** Rich live longer → surviving sample looks richer over time even if individuals don't accumulate.

**Check:** Compare survivors to non-survivors.

**Result:** Non-survivors have lower wealth levels, but similar upward slopes in annualized wealth.

Conclusion: Survivorship not driving the pattern.

## Model Simulations

**What explains rising annualized wealth?**

Test:
1. Uncertain longevity alone → some slow-down, not enough
2. Random medical expenses alone → some rise, too flat
3. Bequest motives alone → maintains wealth, too flat
4. **Longevity + medical + bequests → best fit**

Pattern: Precautionary buffer early (medical risk) + maintain wealth late (bequests).

## Data (HRS 1998-2006)

Sample: 4,630 households age 65+ in 1998, surviving through 2006.

**Demographics (2006):**
- Average age: 80.5
- 37% married, 50% single female, 14% single male
- Mean income: $47k
- 54% high school, 18% college
- 41% fair/poor health
- $2.5k/year OOP medical

## Why This Matters

**Implications:**

If annualized wealth rising:
- Not resource-constrained
- Either precautionary or bequest motives
- Could afford to spend more early in retirement

If falling:
- Resource constraints binding
- Consumption pressure

**Bridge between wealth & consumption:** Panel consumption data rare, annualized wealth provides proxy for consumption possibilities.
