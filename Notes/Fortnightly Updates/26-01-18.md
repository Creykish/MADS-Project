Aside from schmoozing research leads, I have been doing a little bit of research myself. One of the papers that has spurred my interest in this topic was a little paper by two Finnish researchers, Makinen and Toivanen, who optimised a lifetime asset allocation policy which is responsive both to the investor's age and also their realised wealth at a particular age. This is a very novel approach, and would present a major step forward from the other best-to-date piece of research on this topic by Anarkulova et al., who only optimise a policy responsive to age. Time and Wealth are really the only two concrete pieces of information any investor has about the system at any time, so the addition of realised wealth to the factors influencing investment direction means that the asset allocation policy can respond endogenously to the investor’s lived path through the state space, rather than prescribing a one-size-fits-all glidepath. In practical terms, this allows the policy to adapt to good and bad fortune alike: de-risking more aggressively when realised wealth exceeds expectations, and conversely permitting higher risk exposure when outcomes have been unfavourable but the remaining horizon still allows recovery. This state-dependent flexibility is especially appealing in a retirement context, where sequence-of-returns risk looms large and where rigid, age-only rules can be actively harmful. By conditioning on both time and wealth, the policy better reflects the reality of decision-making under uncertainty, and provides a more coherent bridge between normative lifecycle theory and the heuristics that practitioners and retirees already (often implicitly) employ.

All this is to say, in far too many words, is that Makinen and Toivanen's method is very appealing and merits close examination (and explanation). To create a asset allocation policy responsive to time and wealth-at-time, they make use of a control matrix to discretise a policy function $p(t,W)$ which responds to wealth and time. 

This control matrix essentially consists of a time-wealth grid of asset allocations. The authors then use bi-linear interpolation to produce a continuous asset allocation policy based on this discrete grid. 

From this discretised representation, the optimisation problem is framed as choosing the values of the control matrix so as to maximise a given objective functional. Wealth dynamics are simulated forward in time under this policy using Monte Carlo methods, generating a large ensemble of possible lifetime wealth paths. The objective is then evaluated over these simulated paths as a trade-off between expected terminal wealth and some measure of risk, and the policy is iteratively improved. Makinen and Toivanen use the variance and semi-variance (only downside variance) of terminal wealth outcomes as their risk measures in the paper.

A key technical contribution of Makinen and Toivanen is how they perform this optimisation efficiently despite the very high dimensionality of the control matrix. Rather than relying on naïve finite-difference gradients (which would be computationally prohibitive) they employ an adjoint (or reverse-mode) method to compute gradients of the objective with respect to every element of the control matrix in a single backward pass. This makes it feasible to optimise a policy that varies across both time and wealth, even when the grid is relatively fine and the number of policy parameters is large.

I spent some time producing a replication of their work and I am pretty much satisfied with my understanding of what they have done. This can be found in the repo under `Code/replication_makinen.ipynb`. I focused mainly on the two-asset case, where you have one safe asset and one risky asset (i.e. equities). The values in the 2-dimensional control matrix represent the allocation to the risky asset at a particular time and wealth.

As with Makinen and Toivanen's original paper, we simulate returns from the risky asset by sampling from a normal distribution. I use identical simualtion parameters to what they list in their paper. We do this to produce 100,000 random return trajectories. Our goal then is to construct our control matrix to maximise our ultility-risk measure. We do this by using adjoint calculus to calculate the gradient of the utility function in terms of the asset allocation for all gridded policy parameters. We then use L-BFGS-B optimiser, limiting asset allocations to between 0% and 100% of wealth, to iteratively optimise our control matrix. This gives you a solution that looks like this:

This replication of their work reveals a few limitations of this method, which means I will probably need to pursue a different optimisation method if I want to apply it to my problem. The adjoint method means that the current method is limited to utility functions that target only the terminal wealth of the simulation, which is not what we want in a retirement context, where investors are often more concerned with whole-life path-dependent outcomes: the risk of ruin at any point in retirement, sustained periods of low wealth, or the volatility of consumption over the whole lifecycle, rather than just the distribution of wealth at the terminal date.

Additionally, the risk measure they actually use is... weird (or at least hard to interpret). It closely resembles the risk-return measure popularised by Modern Portfolio Theory, but its pretty much impossible to explain what having a $\lambda = 0.3$ tolerance to semi-variance of terminal wealth actually means to an investor. What you'd actually with this is to compute an "efficient frontier" or a set of policy settings that produce the maximum return for a given risk level and then have to select the setting that best represents you're investors goals, but even this is very limited and is variance is just a questionable choice of risk measure for cases where investors are withdrawing money from their portfolio (as the variance of an ensemble of portfolios who's terminal wealth is all $0 is 0, so what does that even measure?).
