The minimum level of consumption or standard of living below which a household cannot fall, either because of biological necessity, social norms, or policy guarantees.

**Why it matters for lifecycle modelling:**
A consumption floor changes the effective utility function. Below the floor, marginal utility is very high (or infinite). This creates an effective kink — households will take costly actions (hold precautionary savings, accept lower returns) to avoid breaching it.

**De Nardi et al. (2010) result:**
Removing the consumption floor from their model causes simulated median assets to *rise* from $150k to $220k (age 74-81), because without the floor, households fear catastrophic outcomes and hold even more precautionary wealth. The floor actually *reduces* precautionary saving by bounding the downside.

**NZ floor components:**
1. **NZ Super:** $27,013/year single / $41,557/year couple — universal, not means-tested
2. **Public healthcare:** GP, hospital, prescriptions largely free at point of use
3. **Residential care subsidy:** Asset-tested at ~$240k in financial assets (home usually exempt)
4. **Food subsidies, community services card**

The NZ floor is high relative to most countries. For No Frills provincial retirees, NZ Super alone nearly covers all spending. This should significantly reduce precautionary saving relative to the US benchmark.

**Interaction with optimal asset allocation:**
A high floor → lower downside risk → higher optimal equity allocation. If the worst-case scenario (wealth = 0) still provides $27k/year via NZ Super, the retiree can tolerate more portfolio volatility than in a country with no guaranteed floor.

**Means-testing kink:**
Residential care subsidy is asset-tested at ~$240k. This creates a potential implicit tax on savings above this threshold (marginal benefit of extra savings = 0 if they'll just fund care that would otherwise be subsidised). Worth modelling for the relevant wealth range.
