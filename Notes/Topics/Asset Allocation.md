Asset allocation is an investment strategy that divides a portfolio among different asset categories, such as stocks, bonds, and cash, to balance risk and reward. See [[Risk Return Tradeoff of Assets]].

## Why It Matters

**Ibbotson & Kaplan (2001):** [[Asset Allocation Explains Fund Performance]] explains ~90% of variation in fund returns over time. Security selection and market timing explain very little. This makes asset allocation the primary lever for improving outcomes.

## Asset Classes

- **Equities (stocks):** Higher expected return, higher short-term volatility, but mean-reverts at long horizons (variance ratio 0.75 at 30 years -> safer for long-horizon investors than short-run statistics suggest)
- **Bonds (fixed income):** Lower expected return, low short-term volatility, BUT variance ratio 2.30 at 30 years -> gets riskier at long horizons. Poor inflation hedge (corr with inflation = -0.78)
- **Cash/bills:** Near risk-free over short periods; erodes purchasing power in real terms over long periods
- **International equities:** Key [[Block Bootstrap Returns]] finding: international equities provide better long-horizon diversification than domestic bonds
- **Housing:** Large illiquid asset for most NZ households. Behaves differently from financial assets. See [[Blundell et al - Comparing Retirement Wealth US vs England]]

## Lifecycle Dimension

For retirement planning, the key question is how allocation should change over time. See [[Life-cycle Asset Allocation]] for the full treatment.

Key insight: what looks like a well-diversified portfolio (60% stocks, 40% bonds) using short-run statistics is actually heavily concentrated in long-run risk, because bonds become riskier than stocks at 30-year horizons.
