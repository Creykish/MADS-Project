The proportion of wealth remaining at death that is transferred to heirs, charities, or other beneficiaries. A potential explanation for why retirees hold more wealth than simple life-cycle models predict.

**Evidence for bequest motives:**
- Hurd (1987): proposed bequests as primary explanation for slow decumulation
- Some survey evidence shows wealthy retirees express desire to leave estates
- Bequest utility appears in several structural lifecycle models as a way to fit slow drawdown

**Evidence against bequest motives as the *primary* driver:**
- Alonso-García et al. (2022, Australia & Netherlands): bequest motive "not important in either country, except for inter vivos transfers to a surviving spouse"
- Retirees prioritise financial independence and quality of life, not leaving estates
- De Nardi et al. (2010): model without bequest motive, using only medical expense risk, fits slow drawdown of US data well
- RIIG (2024, NZ): limited drawdown more consistent with precautionary motives and lifestyle adjustment than intentional bequests

**The identification problem:**
Bequest motives and precautionary saving both predict slow drawdown. Hard to distinguish because:
- Retirees who want to leave money will also hold precautionary buffers
- Surveys measure stated preferences, not revealed preferences
- Accidental bequests arise mechanically from uncertain longevity even without a bequest motive

**NZ context:**
- 66% of NZ retirees own homes outright — creates large "accidental" bequests from housing
- High home ownership may generate bequest-like behaviour (reluctance to downsize) even without explicit motive
- NZ Super eliminates the "poverty in old age" fear that might otherwise justify precautionary saving for survival — leaving more room for bequest-motivated wealth holding
