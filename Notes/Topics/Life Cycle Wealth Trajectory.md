The time series of an investor's total wealth over the course of their lifetime. Standard life-cycle theory predicts a hump-shaped trajectory: rising during working years, peaking around retirement, then declining through decumulation.

**Standard prediction:**
Wealth rises during accumulation (contributions + returns), peaks at retirement, then falls as withdrawals fund consumption until death (and terminal wealth = 0 if no bequest motive).

**What actually happens:**
The empirical trajectory is far flatter in retirement than theory predicts:
- Love, Palumbo & Smith (2009): "annualized comprehensive wealth" actually **rises 30%** between ages 70-75 in the US (1998-2006)
- Grattan Institute (2019, Australia): fewer than half of retirees draw down assets at all; over 40% are net savers
- De Nardi et al. (2010): rich elderly run down assets slowest — opposite of what simple model predicts

**Measurement matters:**
Love et al. use "annualized comprehensive wealth" = (financial + housing + Social Security + pensions) ÷ expected years ÷ household size. This is the right measure — raw wealth declines but expected remaining years fall even faster, so the per-year amount rises.

**Why wealth doesn't decline as expected:**
See [[Retirees Spend Less in Retirement than Predicted by Theory]]:
- Precautionary saving for uncertain [[Medical Expenses]] and longevity
- Mortality selection: only healthier/wealthier households survive to old ages
- Public consumption floor ([[Pensions & Social Security]]) reduces the need to spend down
- Possible [[Bequeathment]] motives
- Voluntary spending decline — retirees *choose* to spend less as mobility/preferences change

**NZ implications:**
With NZ Super as guaranteed floor, many NZ retirees (especially modest spenders) may see wealth trajectory that *stays flat or rises* in nominal terms. Financial assets may only need to fund 10-15 years of drawdown before NZ Super fully covers spending (as real spending declines ~2%/year).
