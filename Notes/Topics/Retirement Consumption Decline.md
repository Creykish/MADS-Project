# Retirement Consumption Decline

One of the central empirical questions in retirement finance: how much does real consumption fall after retirement, why does it fall, and what are the implications for how much wealth retirees actually need?

## How Much Does Spending Fall?

**NZ / Actuarial evidence (RIIG 2024):**
- Real spending declines approximately 2%/year after age 65 on average
- Implication: $1 of spending at 65 becomes ~$0.67 in real terms by 85 (20 years × 2%/year)
- A savings target built assuming constant real spending **overstates** required wealth by ~60% ($605k vs $375k for a 2% decline)

**Australian evidence (Grattan Institute 2019):**
- Retirees' spending declines 0.5–1.0%/year in real terms
- Over 40% of retirees are net savers throughout retirement
- Fewer than 1 in 5 retirees draw down their assets by more than 25% before death

**NZ survey evidence (Le & Richardson 2019):**
- Mean household expenditure drops 44% from pre-retirement peak to age 65
- This partially reflects cohort effects (peak earner years), not just age effect
- Continued decline post-65 consistent with RIIG trajectory

**UK/US comparison (Banks et al. 2016):**
- UK: real non-durable consumption falls ~2.2%/year
- US: real non-durable consumption falls ~1.4%/year
- US decline slower due to elevated medical expense uncertainty constraining the ability to freely spend down

## Why Does Spending Fall?

**Voluntary / demand-side explanations:**
- Tastes change: less travel, dining out, new clothing as mobility declines
- "Big ticket" spending in early retirement (travel, home renovations) tapers by mid-retirement
- Substitution of time for money: retirees cook at home, self-service
- Reduced commuting and work-related costs

**Involuntary / health-driven:**
- Health shocks reduce ability to engage in active leisure
- Cognitive decline may reduce ability to plan or execute spending
- Late-life care costs partially offset the decline (but are counted separately in NZ Super / RIIG analysis)

**Precautionary / behavioural:**
- Retirees hold back spending "just in case" even when financially comfortable
- Survey evidence (Alonso-García et al. 2022): Australian and Dutch retirees primarily cite precautionary motives and desire for financial independence, not bequests

## Implications for Savings Benchmarks

If spending declines 2%/year in real terms, the present value of a retirement income stream is substantially lower than a constant-spending assumption implies.

**RIIG savings target comparison (6% real return, age 65→90):**
- Constant real spending: **$605k**
- 2% real decline per year: **$375k** (38% lower)
- 1% real decline: **$480k**
- 2.5% real decline: **$328k**

This means conventional financial planning rules that assume constant real withdrawals systematically overstate the wealth needed for a comfortable retirement.

**NZ Super interaction:**
- As spending declines and NZ Super stays roughly inflation-adjusted, the gap between spending and NZ Super narrows over time
- For No Frills provincial retirees: drawdown may become near-zero within 10–15 years of retirement
- This compresses the effective drawdown period even further, reducing the required savings pool

## Voluntary vs Involuntary Decline — The Debate

This is contested. Key distinction matters for modelling:

- **If voluntary**: model as utility-maximising choice — lower spending *is* optimal, wealth needed is lower, standard life-cycle analysis applies
- **If involuntary (health-constrained)**: retirees *want* to spend but can't — welfare loss, different policy implications, savings benchmarks based on desired spending should not be discounted by the decline
- **If precautionary**: retirees *could* spend but choose not to — similar to voluntary but driven by risk aversion rather than true preference change

Evidence leans toward **primarily voluntary + precautionary** in NZ context (RIIG; Alonso-García), but the debate is not settled in the US/UK literature.
