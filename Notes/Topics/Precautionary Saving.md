Savings maintained as a buffer against uncertain future costs — particularly [[Medical Expenses]], long-term care, and uncertain longevity. Households hold more wealth than the simple life-cycle model predicts because they face risks that are hard to insure against.

**Mechanism:** If there is a large possible future expense with unknown timing, a risk-averse agent will hold a buffer stock above the simple life-cycle optimum. The buffer size depends on risk aversion (γ), the size and variance of the expense, and the availability of public insurance.

**Evidence for precautionary motives:**
- Palumbo (1999): accounting for uncertain medical costs explains much of the slow asset decline
- De Nardi, French & Jones (2010): medical expenses that rise steeply with age and income explain why rich elderly run down assets slowest
- Alonso-García et al. (2022): Australian and Dutch retirees cite precautionary motives as primary reason for not spending down
- Banks et al. (2016): US retirees reduce real consumption more slowly than UK retirees — driven by higher and more uncertain out-of-pocket medical costs

**NZ context:**
Precautionary motives in NZ are probably weaker than the US but present:
- Public healthcare covers most core medical costs
- Residential care is publicly subsidised below ~$240k asset threshold
- NZ Super provides a guaranteed inflation-linked income floor
- RIIG (2024): "no evidence to suggest New Zealand would see widespread holding back of savings" for precautionary reasons

But late-life care (dementia, residential facilities) still involves genuine cost uncertainty — ~53% of NZ retirees projected to use residential care by 2040.

**Interaction with [[Consumption Floor]]:**
A strong public consumption floor (NZ Super + healthcare + care subsidies) reduces the precautionary motive relative to countries like the US. When the floor is high, the downside risk of spending freely is low.
