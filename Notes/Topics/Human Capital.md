Human capital is the discounted present value of future wages.

$$
H = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{E[w_t]}{(1+r)^t}
$$

## Bond-like Properties

For most workers, human capital behaves like a long-duration bond:
- Wages are relatively stable and predictable (especially salaried workers)
- Low correlation with stock market returns
- Depletes deterministically as the worker ages toward retirement
- This makes human capital substitute for bonds in the total-wealth portfolio

**Implication for [[Life-cycle Asset Allocation]] (Cocco 2005):**
- Young investor: total wealth = small financial portfolio + very large human capital
- The implicit portfolio is already heavily "bond-like"
- To reach the optimal total-portfolio risk level, financial portfolio must be heavily equity
- As years of future wages diminish, equity share should fall
- -> the human capital argument justifies [[Decreasing Equity Strategy]]

## Labor Flexibility Amplifier

([[Merton & Bodie - Labor supply flexibility and portfolio choice in a life cycle model]] 1992)
- Investors can work more hours or delay retirement to recover from investment losses
- This labor hedge makes the effective human capital larger and more bond-like
- -> greater flexibility -> more equity risk-taking in financial portfolio
- Conversely: rigid labor contracts, near mandatory retirement -> less flexibility -> less equity

## Human Capital Risk

Not all human capital is bond-like:
- Finance/banking worker: wages correlate with stock market -> risky human capital
- Commission salesperson: wages volatile -> risky human capital
- Civil servant: very stable wages -> more bond-like than average

([[Merton & Bodie - Labor supply flexibility and portfolio choice in a life cycle model]]):
- The riskier the human capital, the **lower** the financial investment in risky assets
- Risky HC and equity are complements in total risk -> financial portfolio must hedge

## Limits of the Argument

**[[Anarkulova et al - Beyond the Status Quo (Critical Assessment of Lifecycle Advice)]] (2025) challenge:**
- Even if bonds are appropriate replacements for declining human capital, bonds fail at long horizons
- Variance ratio of bonds at 30 years = 2.30 -> bonds get riskier, not safer, over time
- -> the asset that should replace human capital (bonds) is actually a bad choice
- International stocks provide better diversification than bonds at long horizons
- Conclusion: the declining-equity conclusion from human capital theory doesn't hold if bonds are suboptimal

## NZ Super as Human Capital Replacement

- From age 65, [[Pensions & Social Security]] (NZ Super) pays ~$28,900/year (single, after tax)
- PV of NZ Super from age 65 at 2% real discount rate over 25 years ~ $550-800k
- This acts as a bond-like riskless annuity, replacing part of the role bonds play
- -> financial portfolio can remain equity-heavy even in retirement because the "safe" allocation is already provided
