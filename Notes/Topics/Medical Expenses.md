Out-of-pocket healthcare and long-term care costs faced by retirees. A key driver of [[Precautionary Saving]] and slow asset decumulation, especially in countries with limited public coverage.

**US evidence (De Nardi, French & Jones 2010):**
- Age 70: ~$1,200/year (bad-health woman)
- Age 100: ~$19,000/year
- Age 95, 20th income pct: $2,700/year
- Age 95, 80th income pct: $16,000/year
- **Medical expenses are a luxury good** — rich retirees spend far more out-of-pocket
- Top 5% of spenders account for 35% of total costs, 49% of OOP costs

**Cross-country comparison:**
- UK: real non-durable spending falls 2.2%/year — precautionary motive "negligible" due to NHS
- US: real spending falls only 1.4%/year — retirees hold savings to cover future healthcare costs
- The ~0.8ppt/year difference attributed to medical expense uncertainty in the US

**NZ context:**
- Core medical services publicly funded (GP visits, hospital, prescriptions)
- Residential care: publicly subsidised if financial assets < ~$240k (home usually exempt)
- Estimated 53% of NZ retirees aged 65+ will use residential care before death (projected 2040)
- Median entry to residential care: age 85, median stay 18 months
- Care costs uncertain in terms of duration, type, and level of subsidy — genuine tail risk even under public system

**Modelling implications:**
- In NZ: medical expenses matter less than in US; consumption floor from public system is high
- But late-life care tail risk is real — worth modelling as a stochastic shock at advanced ages
- De Nardi et al. show the **average level** of medical costs matters more than the risk component for asset trajectories
