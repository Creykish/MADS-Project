US equity return history overstates what a typical investor in a typical country should expect going forward. The US had an exceptional 20th century. Using US-only historical returns to calibrate lifecycle simulations produces upward-biased outcomes.

## Evidence

**[[Shiller - Life-cycle personal accounts proposal for Social Security]] (2006):**
- US geometric avg real stock return 1900-2000: **6.8%**
- 15-country international median (Dimson, Marsh & Staunton 2002): **4.8%** -> gap of 2.2pp
- WSJ economists survey expected 44-year real stock return: **4.6%** -> matches international, not US
- Bond gap even larger: US bonds 1.5% vs international median 1.2%
- Brown, Goetzmann & Jorion (1999): 39-country sample, US capital appreciation 5%/yr vs median **1.5%**/yr

**Consequence for lifecycle simulations:**
- Shiller's baseline lifecycle (85->15% equity): loss prob 32% with US history, **71% with international-adjusted returns**
- Baseline lifecycle median net value: positive with US history, *negative* with international-adjusted
- All-equity: loss prob 2% -> 33% when stock returns drop 2.2pp
- The headline optimism of lifecycle simulations using US data is substantially a product of US exceptionalism

**[[Anarkulova et al - Beyond the Status Quo (Critical Assessment of Lifecycle Advice)]] (2025):**
- Explicitly uses global data (block bootstrap from international equity + bond series) to avoid US survivorship bias
- This is one of the methodological improvements over prior work
- All-equity result is robust to international return experience

## Why The US Was Different

- Strong 20th century GDP growth (US grew faster than international consensus projected)
- No wars fought on US soil (unlike most of the 15-country sample)
- Strong institutional development and rule of law
- Survivor bias: countries with catastrophic outcomes (capital controls, nationalisation, hyperinflation) may be excluded or underweighted

## Implication for NZ

NZ is a small, open economy with a market cap ~0.1% of global total. NZ equity has higher return variance and concentration risk than international equity. NZ historical equity return data is limited and less reliable. -> Lifecycle models for NZ should use **international** return parameters, not US-only, and should weight international equity heavily in the asset universe.
