# Spending Decline is Primarily Voluntary

The observed real decline in retiree spending is largely a product of voluntary preference change and lifestyle adjustment, not financial constraint or forced frugality. Retirees *choose* to spend less as their priorities, health, and activity levels change.

## Evidence

**Grattan Institute (2019, Australia):**
- Over 40% of retirees are net savers throughout retirement
- Self-reported financial comfort is high among retirees who don't draw down
- Most retirees feel financially comfortable and *choose* not to spend their savings, rather than being prevented from doing so
- Framing: "retirees are more likely to leave an estate than exhaust their savings"

**RIIG (2024, NZ):**
- "No evidence to suggest New Zealand would see widespread holding back of savings" due to precautionary reasons
- Real spending declining 2%/year is consistent with voluntary lifestyle adjustment
- Spending decline pattern tracks reduced activity, travel, and large discretionary purchases — not rising costs

**Alonso-García et al. (2022, Australia & Netherlands):**
- Primary motives for not drawing down: desire for financial independence, wanting to "enjoy life now as well as later"
- Not primarily fear of future costs or forced saving
- Interpretation: conservative spending *is* the preferred lifestyle, not a constraint

**Banks, Blundell et al. (UK context):**
- UK retirees (NHS covers medical costs) show spending declines similar to or greater than US retirees
- If precautionary saving for medical costs were driving slow drawdown, UK should show faster decline than US — yet decline is similar or faster in UK
- Consistent with demand-side / preference explanation for spending decline

## Mechanism

As retirees age:
- Travel and active leisure become less desirable or possible
- Social spending (restaurants, events) declines as social circles narrow
- Replacement spending falls: fewer new clothes, cars, home goods needed
- Time becomes a substitute for money in many activities

The standard life-cycle model treats preferences as constant. If preferences genuinely shift toward lower spending with age, then the "underspending puzzle" is not a puzzle — it's rational.

## Counter-evidence

- Palumbo (1999): even optimal precautionary-saving model underpredicts the actual degree of wealth retention — suggests genuine conservatism beyond pure preference change
- Heimer et al. (2015): retirees overestimate longevity → hold savings "just in case" even when not needed — a behavioural rather than purely preference explanation
- Some portion of decline may be involuntary (health-driven inability to consume)

## Implications for Modelling

If decline is primarily voluntary:
- Calibrate consumption trajectory as declining 1–2%/year in real terms from age 65
- This is a preference, not a constraint — welfare analysis should treat lower spending as *good* (higher utility), not as a problem to be avoided
- Savings benchmarks should use the declining trajectory, not constant-real assumptions
