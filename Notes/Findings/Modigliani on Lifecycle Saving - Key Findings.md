## Lifecycle Accumulation is Central to Wealth Building

**Finding:** The Life Cycle Hypothesis demonstrates that ~75–80% of wealth in developed economies is self-accumulated through lifecycle saving during working years, with only ~20–25% inherited.

**From:** Modigliani [1985]; Ando & Kennickell [1985]; Menchik & David [1983] (probate data)

**Mechanism:**
- Households smooth consumption over finite life by saving during earning years and dissaving in retirement
- This "hump saving" creates a natural wealth accumulation path independent of per capita income level
- The wealth-income ratio is determined primarily by retirement length (M), not by bequest motive

**Evidence:**
- U.S. wealth-income ratio ~4–5 matches prediction of pure LCH for M≈10 years, no bequests
- Peak wealth observed at age 60–65 (matches theory)
- Cohorts' wealth accumulation proportional to relative life resources, not absolute income

## NZ Super Reduces Private Saving Need

**Finding:** A guaranteed income floor (annuity-like) acts as implicit wealth in the lifecycle framework, reducing the financial wealth households must accumulate at retirement.

**From:** Modigliani LCH logic applied to social insurance programs

**Mechanism:**
- NZ Super provides ~$33k/year (single) to ~$51k/year (couple) inflation-linked
- In lifecycle terms, this is equivalent to holding a large bond/annuity
- Also hedged to inflation - which is even better than bonds (which are negatively correlated).
- Reduces required drawdown from financial portfolio
- model NZ Super as exogenous guaranteed income floor
- Justifies potentially higher equity allocation in financial assets (no need for bond buffer since income floor is safe)
- Anarkulova [2025] all-equity result becomes more plausible with NZ Super providing "bond substitute"

## Mortality and Precautionary Motives Explain Slow Decumulation

**Finding:** Observed slow dissaving by retirees (wealth doesn't decline linearly to zero) is consistent with lifecycle theory, explained by two mechanisms.

**From:** Modigliani [1985]; Davies [1981]; Hurd [1986]

**Two Mechanisms:**

1. **Precautionary saving against mortality risk:**
   - Households don't know when they'll die
   - Perfect annuity markets don't exist (adverse selection, high cost)
   - Rational response: hold buffer wealth; die with non-zero estate
   - This is NOT a bequest motive, just prudence
   - Effect grows with age (uncertainty increases in cost of running out of wealth)

2. **Bequest motive (concentrated at top):**
   - Only ~3% of population cite bequest as saving reason
   - ~33% of top wealth decile do
   - Menchik & David: bequests rise sharply above 80th percentile of life resources
   - Much smaller aggregate effect than precaution

**Implication for Your Research:**
- Your spending decline (2%/year) implies rising consumption needs relative to wealth
- At some point households may become wealth-constrained (can't meet desired spending)
- Precautionary motive means they hold excess wealth as buffer
- Optimal glidepath should account for both consumption smoothing AND precaution

**For NZ Context:**
- Long-term care costs (not public) create real precautionary need
- NZ health system covers acute care but not residential care (test at ~$240k for couples)
- Households should hold buffer for care costs—not just for mortality uncertainty

## Bequest Motive is Concentrated in Top Decile

**Finding:** The pure bequest motive (desire to leave an estate) is limited to wealthy households and does NOT explain most wealth accumulation.

**From:** Projector & Weiss [1964]; Menchik & David [1983]; Hurd [1986]

**Evidence:**

| Income Bracket | Cite Bequest as Reason for Saving |
|---|---|
| Bottom 90% | ~3% |
| Top 10% | ~33% |

Menchik & David probate study:
- Bequests small (<80th percentile of life resources, essentially zero)
- Above 80th percentile: bequests rise rapidly with (permanent) income
- Strong evidence bequest share rises with relative position in cohort distribution

Hurd [1986] panel data test:
- Households with children expected to have more wealth + lower dissaving if bequest motive strong
- **Finding:** Households with children have *less* wealth and dissave at same rate as childless households
- Suggests bequest motive is not quantitatively important

**Implication for Your Research:**
- Don't over-weight bequest motive in your model
- NZ data on retirement spending + wealth suggests similar pattern (most wealth is consumed)
- Policy should not assume bequest motive is a major constraint on retiree spending

**Policy Note:** The low importance of bequests means government policies that encourage consumption in retirement (flexible withdrawal rules, downsize incentives) don't face major behavioral resistance from bequest concerns.

## Interest Rates Have Small Effect on Saving

**Finding:** The relationship between interest rates and aggregate saving is empirically weak and theoretically ambiguous.

**From:** Modigliani [1975]; Friedman & Warshawsky [1985]

**Theoretical Mechanism:**

When interest rate rises:
- **Income effect:** Higher returns → higher lifetime income → higher consumption → *lower* saving
- **Substitution effect:** Future consumption more expensive → *higher* saving

**Empirical finding:** These offset, leaving **saving rate largely independent of r** (over reasonable range)

Partial exception: Pension saving through annuities may show negative correlation with interest rates in short run (workers save less if annuity payouts fall) but positive in long run.

**Implication for Your Research:**
- Assumption: Interest rates don't mechanically drive saving or glidepath choice
- Optimal allocation responds to returns (through expected-return calculations), not to behavioral saving response
- Your optimization framework should NOT assume agents save more when equity risk premium rises

**For NZ Context:**
- Central bank rate changes affect mortgage rates but not necessarily lifecycle saving behavior
- KiwiSaver contribution rates are exogenous (policy), not responding strongly to interest rates
- Expected returns on equity (not interest rates) drive asset allocation choices

## Growth Rate Predicts National Saving, Not Income Level

**Finding:** The aggregate saving rate is determined by the growth rate of the economy, NOT by per capita income or perceived "thrift."

**From:** Modigliani LCH Proposition 1–3

**Mechanism (Neisser & Bentzel effects):**

When economy grows:
- Younger workers earn more than older retired cohorts
- Young save, old dissave, but young savers > old dissavers (more numerous + higher wealth)
- Net result: positive saving
- Saving rate ≈ (M/2) × growth rate (where M = retirement length)

**Formula:**
- 2% growth → ~4% saving rate (for 10-year retirement)
- 4% growth → ~8% saving rate
- 0% growth → 0% saving (dissavers offset savers exactly)

**Implication for Your Research:**
- NZ growth ~2–2.5% → expect ~5–6% national saving rate (consistent with data?)
- Policy changes (tax incentives, KiwiSaver, etc.) don't fundamentally override growth-driven saving
- But they can shift *distribution* of saving (who saves, in what instruments)

**For Your NZ Study:**
- Document actual NZ household saving rate by life stage
- Test whether it matches LCH prediction for 2–2.5% growth + 20-year retirement horizon
- If not, investigate what's driving deviation (policy, uncertainty, credit constraints)

## Finite Life and Retirement Length are Key Parameters

**Finding:** The length of retirement (M) is the dominant determinant of the wealth-income ratio and saving rate under lifecycle assumptions.

**From:** Modigliani LCH Proposition 6

**Formula:**
$$W/Y = \frac{M}{2} \quad \text{(stationary economy)}$$

**Examples:**
- M = 10 years → W/Y = 5
- M = 15 years → W/Y = 7.5
- M = 20 years → W/Y = 10

**For NZ:**
- Average life expectancy 82, retirement 65 → M ≈ 17 years
- W/Y should be ~8.5 under pure LCH
- But actual NZ retiree wealth may be lower due to credit constraints, myopia

**Implication for Your Research:**
- Changes to retirement age directly affect optimal allocation
- If NZ raises retirement age to 67 (M = 15), reduces required W/Y ratio
- This has large behavioral effect—less saving pressure on workers, less decumulation pressure on retirees
- Your model should be transparent about M as a sensitivity parameter

## Relation to Main Glidepath Debate

| LCH Prediction | How Debate Authors Challenge It |
|---|---|
| **Constant consumption** → flat risky-asset share (Merton) | Estrada: real sequence-of-returns risk makes allocation time-varying; Pfau: IID assumptions miss autocorrelation |
| **Hump-shaped wealth path** | Parker: DRL shows actual optimal paths are nearly flat post-retirement (not declining as classical LCH + dissaving would suggest) |
| **Finite-life retirement dissaving** | Observed dissaving is slow, suggesting precaution or bequest motive, OR medical costs/longevity uncertainty that LCH doesn't fully model |
| **Interest rates don't affect saving** | Consistent with lifecycle optimization, but asset allocation *does* depend on expected returns and market conditions |
