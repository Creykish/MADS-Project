## Finding

A 4% first-year real withdrawal rate (i.e. withdraw 4% of initial portfolio value, then inflation-adjust each subsequent year) is the maximum "safe" rate for a 30-year retirement under all historical US market conditions since 1926. No historical cohort retiring with a 50-75% equity portfolio exhausted funds within 33 years at this rate.

## Evidence

- [[Bengen - Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data]] (1994): original derivation; 4% survives all cohorts including 1973-74 "Big Bang"; 4.25% can fail in 28 years; 5% fails for late-1960s/early-1970s retirees within 20 years
- [[Blanchett - Dynamic Allocation Strategies for Distribution Portfolios]] (2007): confirms the 4% baseline; shows 3.58% failure probability at 4% WR with 60/40 static portfolio; fee drag is the main risk (1.5%/yr fees effectively raises WR to 5.5%)
- [[Pfau & Kitces - Reducing Retirement Risk with a Rising Equity Glide Path]] (2014): uses 4% and 5% as the two benchmark withdrawal rates in their rising-equity glidepath analysis
- [[Estrada - The Retirement Glidepath]] (2015): uses 4% as the standard withdrawal rate in 19-country rolling-period analysis

## Mechanism

Standard sequence-of-returns risk argument: a bad early period (falling markets + high inflation simultaneously) depletes the portfolio permanently because real withdrawals continue while the portfolio value is compressed. Using long-run average returns to compute withdrawal capacity misses this - the "average" planner gets caught out when an event like 1973-74 occurs.

The 4% rule is conservative enough to survive the worst historical US event (1973-74 stagflation) with margin.

## Caveats and challenges

- US-only data: Bengen used US equity returns (survivorship-biased; international median is ~2pp lower). The rule may not hold for non-US retirees or in the future -> see [[US Equity Returns Show Survivorship Bias Relative to International Experience]]
- Optimal equity range matters: Bengen found 50-75% equity is required; bonds-heavy portfolios fail even at 4%
- Fees not accounted for in the original formulation: 1% total fees effectively reduces safe withdrawal to ~3%
- Sequence risk in early years means even 4% is fragile if returns are very bad in the first 5-10 years
- Does not account for flexible/dynamic withdrawals - a retiree who can cut spending in bad years can sustain higher average withdrawals

## NZ Context

NZ Super provides a base income floor reducing reliance on portfolio withdrawals -> the 4% rule is less binding for most NZ retirees. Those with NZ Super covering basic living costs can run a higher effective withdrawal rate from their investment portfolio for discretionary spending without risk of complete depletion.
