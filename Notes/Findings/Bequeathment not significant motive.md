[[Bequeathment]] is not a significant motive for the majority of retirees in OECD countries.

## Evidence

**Australia & Netherlands (Alonso-García et al. 2022):**
> "Similarly the *intended bequest* motive is not important in either country, except for *inter vivos* transfers to a surviving spouse, which is more highly ranked in the Netherlands in line with expectations."

Retires in both countries primarily want to: remain financially independent, enjoy their remaining years, and protect against future costs. Leaving money to children ranks low.

**NZ (RIIG 2024):**
- Limited evidence of bequest-driven wealth accumulation in NZ retirees
- Slow drawdown more consistent with precautionary motives and lifestyle adjustment

**De Nardi, French & Jones (2010):**
- Model that *excludes* bequest motive but includes medical expense risk fits US data well
- Suggests bequests are not needed to explain slow drawdown

**Methodological note:**
Most studies find bequests matter only in *special cases*:
- Inter vivos transfers to spouses
- Very wealthy households where assets far exceed plausible medical/longevity needs
- Strategic bequests (exchanging inheritance for care/attention from children)

## Counter-argument
See [[Bequeathment a significant motive]] for evidence that bequest motives do matter at the top of the wealth distribution or when properly modelled with structural methods.
