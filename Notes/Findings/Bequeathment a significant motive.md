Bequest motives are a significant and distinct driver of retirement saving for some retirees, particularly those with high wealth or strong family ties.

## Evidence

**Hurd (1987, 1989):**
- Early structural work: proposed bequests as primary explanation for slow asset decumulation among US elderly
- Parents with children hold more assets than childless households at the same income/health level
- Interpreted as intentional bequest motive

**Love, Palumbo & Smith (2009):**
- Structural model with bequest utility fits US data better than model without
- Bequest motive needed to explain why the very wealthy (top wealth quintile) maintain near-flat asset profiles into their 80s

**High-wealth retirees (De Nardi et al. 2016 survey):**
- Top income quintile: ~$200k in financial assets at age 74, and they hold onto it
- Standard longevity + medical risk cannot fully explain the very slow drawdown of the richest households
- Some residual wealth holding consistent with intentional bequests

**Housing channel (NZ-specific):**
- 66% of NZ retirees own their home outright
- Strong reluctance to downsize or access home equity — consistent with desire to pass housing wealth to children
- Reverse mortgages remain uncommon in NZ despite policy support (consistent with bequest motive for housing)

## The Identification Problem

Bequests and precautionary saving both predict slow drawdown. The distinction requires:
- Observing *actual* bequests (post-mortem data), not just asset holdings
- Comparing asset trajectories of parents vs non-parents
- Structural modelling with bequest utility term

Most survey-based studies find bequests are not stated as important — but stated preferences may not capture *revealed* preferences for wealth holding.

## Counter-argument
See [[Bequeathment not significant motive]] — survey evidence from Australia and Netherlands finds bequest motive ranked low by most retirees.
