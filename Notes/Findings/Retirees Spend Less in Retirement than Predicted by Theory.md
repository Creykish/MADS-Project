Individuals in retirement spend much less (decumulate wealth much more slowly) than the optimal trajectory determined by the [[The Life Cycle Model of Consumption]]. This has been documented across the US, UK, Australia, NZ, Netherlands, and elsewhere.

## Key Evidence

**US (De Nardi, French & Jones 2010):**
- Over 1/3 of all US wealth is held by households aged 65+
- Balanced panel (survivors only): $84k → $44k over age 74-84 = $40k decline in 10 years
- Rich elderly run down assets the slowest — the *opposite* of what theory predicts
- Model without bequest motive, using only medical + longevity risk, fits the data well

**US (Love, Palumbo & Smith 2009):**
- Annualized comprehensive wealth (total resources ÷ remaining years) actually *rises* +30% from age 70-75 (1998-2006)
- ~50% of HRS sample saw annualized wealth rise >25%; only ~12% saw a decline >25%
- Rising even as raw wealth balances fall — because life expectancy falls faster than assets

**US (Palumbo 1999):**
- Even accounting for optimal precautionary saving for medical costs, actual retirees save more than optimal — real-world behaviour is even more conservative than the model predicts

**Australia/Netherlands (Alonso-García et al. 2022):**
- Survey: many retirees hold assets or keep saving well into old age
- Primary motives: financial independence, being able to "enjoy life now and later"
- Precautionary motive for medical costs important if health/mortality shocks anticipated
- Bequest motive largely absent (see [[Bequeathment not significant motive]])

**NZ (RIIG 2024):**
- Real spending declines ~2%/year after 65
- Many NZ retirees maintain or grow nominal wealth throughout retirement
- This is primarily consistent with voluntary spending reduction and lifestyle adjustment

**NZ (Le & Richardson 2019):**
- Mean household expenditure 44% lower at age 65+ than peak working-age spending
- Continued decline post-65 consistent with RIIG trajectory

## Proposed Explanations

1. **[[Precautionary Saving]]** for uncertain [[Medical Expenses]] and longevity risk — primary explanation supported by most structural models
2. **[[Bequeathment]] motives** — proposed by Hurd (1987), but poorly supported by survey evidence
3. **Biased mortality beliefs** — retirees overestimate survival probability → hold more savings "just in case" (Heimer et al. 2015)
4. **Voluntary preference change** — tastes genuinely shift away from spending as activity levels decline (see [[Spending Decline is Primarily Voluntary]])
5. **Means-tested benefit interaction** — spending down assets would reduce eligibility for care subsidies; creates implicit incentive to hold assets

All of this points to effective risk aversion that is higher than standard models calibrate. The policy implication is that standard savings targets (assuming constant real withdrawals) **overstate** the wealth required for retirement — see [[Retirement Consumption Decline]] for magnitudes.
